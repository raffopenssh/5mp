Global gold production grew by only 50% over the past two decades, while its real, inflation-adjusted price skyrocketed by 350%. This massive gap has triggered a modern gold rush across Africa, turning low-yield regions into highly lucrative targets. As a result, unregulated mining is expanding rapidly, putting severe environmental pressure on vital river systems, including those inside protected nature reserves.

A primary example is the Papa River, located northeast of Chinko near the South Sudanese border. A report of active gold mining from Chinko prompted this analysis. The area is exceptionally remote; the nearest camp sits fourteen hours away from any town of at least 5,000 people. Maps from the 1930s reveal that former villages, including Tidi and Ouligui, sit directly on gold-bearing rock. While no active settlements remain within 3 km of these sites today, more than twenty historic village names line the Papa River and its tributaries, where only four small camps currently exist. Our working hypothesis is that miners are returning to these long-abandoned settlement sites - possibly guided by oral history - because historic settlement on gold-bearing ground often highlights deposits that are practical to exploit. A pattern that extends across the border: the highest-ranked mining candidate in South Sudan is a former village situated on gold-bearing rock in the Busseri headwaters above Wau. Notably, four out of five settlements established since 2015 lie within 3 km of the historic villages marked on those 1930s maps. These findings identify clear priorities for field investigation; they do not, however, confirm active mining.

The extraction boom impacts the broader cross-border frontier, including poorly monitored areas spanning the sparsely inhabited wilderness between Radom, Numatina, and Southern National Park. We propose this {{ B.total|musd }}, {{ 'two' if F.years==2 else F.years }}-year priority intervention to secure legal and community protections before informal mining networks entrench themselves and mineral rights are locked in.

We will deploy seasonal ground teams and community wildlife agents to critical locations, working in coordination with the South Sudan Wildlife Service alongside national and regional partners. Three complementary structures will operate across the primary drainage basins:

ECHO Teams (five people: four community scouts and a leader) will provide environmental education, resource monitoring, and continuous dialogue within resident border communities. Each ECHO team is attached to one proposed Community Conservancy.

TANGO Teams (five people) will travel alongside transhumant herders, linking protected-area agreements with grazing rights, water points, veterinary care, and basic medical support. Each TANGO team is attached to one herd corridor and moves from the month the herds arrive until the end of the dry season.

Focal Points (one person each) in {{ fp_y1|join(' and ') }}, and in the second year {{ fp_y2|join(' and ') }}, will manage coordination with county and traditional authorities, supported centrally from Juba. Seasonal migration sets the operating calendar.

EASY - HOW THE AREAS WERE DRAWN AND SELECTED

None of the zones in this plan were drawn by hand. They come from a four-step procedure that any reader can re-run, and every number in this document is printed by that procedure rather than typed.

Step 1 - cutting the ground into pieces. The study area, about 480,000 km2 across South Sudan, the Central African Republic, Sudan and northern Congo, was divided into {{ F.solve.units|num }} units on a 2 km grid. The cuts follow only features a person can stand on and point at: rivers and khors, swamp and lake edges, hill chains, roads, international borders and the district lines of the 1930s Sudan Survey sheets. Where no such feature exists, geological contacts (the change from one rock type to another) were allowed as a last resort and are flagged in every description as "not visible on the ground - needs beacons". Each unit is at least 2,000 ha and its outer boundary carries a legibility score: the share of its length that runs along a named feature.

Step 2 - measuring every piece the same way. Each unit was measured with the same satellite layers used throughout this report: residents from the GHSL built-up rasters (a lower bound, never a census), settlements founded since 2015, fire detections and tracked fire fronts for 2024-26 and the share travelling with the herds, cropland in 2003 and 2019, verified forest clearing since 2020, and modelled gold targets and reported workings. Two further layers were fitted and tested rather than assumed. A conversion model (which land turned from bush to farm or village between 2015 and today) was trained and scored on spatially separate 20 km blocks, reaching an AUC of {{ '%.2f'|format(F.threat.auc) }}, meaning it ranks a converted cell above an unconverted one {{ (F.threat.auc*100)|round|int }} times in 100. A herd-movement model grouped the {{ F.movement.fronts|num }} long-range transhumance fronts into {{ F.movement.bundles|words }} origin-destination routes and estimated for each the ground the herds use (a Brownian-bridge utilisation distribution); fitted on 2023-24 and tested on the unseen {{ F.movement.hold_season }}/{{ (F.movement.hold_season+1)%100 }} season, each route's band captured {{ (F.movement.holdout_min*100)|int }}-{{ (F.movement.holdout_max*100)|int }}% of the following year's movement where chance alone would give 0-{{ (F.movement.null_max*100)|int }}%.

Step 3 - assigning one role to every piece, all at once. Each unit receives exactly one of four classes - park-grade core, wilderness, community conservancy or herd corridor - from a single integer programme solved over all {{ F.solve.units|num }} units together. The classes follow fixed rules with physical meaning: core requires at most 0.02 people per km2, under 0.05% cropland, under 0.5 km2 of clearing per 1,000 km2 since 2020 and no reported working; wilderness allows up to 0.5 people per km2; a corridor must be one continuous route from where a herd route starts to where it ends, holding at least {{ (F.solve.capture*100)|int }}% of that route's measured use; everything else is community land. The solver prefers boundaries on named features, keeps gazetted parks as core, and never puts people inside a park. Its weights were set before the run and were not adjusted afterwards. Where a genuine political choice exists - how much new park land to allow, how much of each herd route to protect - the whole problem was solved again at every setting ({{ F.solve.frontier_runs }} runs) and the trade-off printed: people whose land changes class, hectares of park, kilometres of boundary through open bush. The plan presented here is one row of that table, chosen with the reader able to see what the neighbouring rows would have cost.

Step 4 - placing the teams. Team sites were chosen only among existing settlements of 150 or more people (residence implies water; mapped water was re-checked and is printed for every site). For each zone, three candidate sites were shortlisted by the share of the zone's targets they can reach within four hours by car, motorbike or foot, using OpenStreetMap road travel times. Community zones were ranked for an ECHO team by gold-rush urgency (modelled gold targets, people times conversion risk, and how much park or corridor edge the zone shields); corridor zones for a TANGO team by herd use, herd use near residents, and gold targets in the band. A small open-weights language model (muse-glimmer-30b) was used at two points only: to choose among the three shortlisted sites for each team, given their measured reach, population, road and water figures and a fixed set of rules; and to draft each team's one-sentence and 120-word briefs from the stored zone descriptions. It never classified land, drew a boundary or produced a number, and its stated reason for each site choice is printed beside the choice so it can be checked.

What this procedure cannot do is also stated. Populations are satellite lower bounds. Gold targets are model predictions - the model's top 5% of cells captures {{ (F.gold.skill.capture*100)|int }}% of the {{ F.gold.anchors }} known workings, a {{ '%.1f'|format(F.gold.skill.lift) }}-fold improvement on chance, not a map of mines. The herd model explains where herds go along each route, not whether the network as a whole differs from where the country burns. And the boundaries proposed are candidates for a community to walk, not lines to be gazetted as drawn.
