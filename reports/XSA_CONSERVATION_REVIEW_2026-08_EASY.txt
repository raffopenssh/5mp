================================================================================

        WHAT IS HAPPENING IN THE CHINKO-GARAMBA CORRIDOR?
        A plain-language conservation review, August 2026

        The area: 485,000 km2 where the Central African Republic,
        DR Congo and South Sudan meet - about the size of Spain -
        holding four big protected areas: Chinko, Bili-Uere,
        Garamba and Southern National Park.

        The question: how bad are overgrazing, commercial hunting
        and artisanal mining here - and where exactly?

================================================================================


THE WHOLE STORY IN SIX SENTENCES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every dry season a wall of fire sweeps the region from north to south -
almost 4 million satellite fire detections in two years, nearly all of it
people burning for hunting and pasture, and it peaks every January without
exception. Forests are mostly holding: in 25 years the region lost just a
quarter of one percent of its land to clearing, and only a sliver of that
became farmland. The real change is people: more than half of all the
buildings standing today were built in the last ten years, mostly by
families returning to villages their great-grandparents lived in - villages
we can find by name on maps drawn a century ago. Mining is real but small,
concentrated in two known clusters, and completely invisible to satellites -
no fire, no lights - so only people on the ground can watch it. The single
biggest management gap is Garamba, which stops only 6 in every 100 fires
that enter it. And the single best window to act is the same every year:
mid-December to mid-February, when the burning season arrives everywhere
at once.


1. FIRE - A JANUARY OCEAN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In two years, satellites logged 3,990,865 individual fire detections here,
grouped into 38,725 distinct fires. These are not campfires: an average
fire here burns for 22 days and moves 6.7 km per day. It is a moving front.

Almost none of it is lightning, and almost none of it is planned management
burning (about 300 fires out of 38,725). This is a landscape-scale human
burning culture: hunters driving game, herders renewing pasture.

The year has one shape, and it never changes:

    A YEAR OF FIRE (monthly detections, last full season)

    Sep  2025 |                            997
    Oct  2025 | *                       25,857
    Nov  2025 | *********              230,947
    Dec  2025 | ****************       398,907

    Feb  2026 | ********               187,834
    Mar  2026 | *****                  127,078
    Apr  2026 | *                       19,229
    May  2026 |                          5,993

Nov-Feb carries about 87% of the whole year's fire. July-September is
essentially fire-free. The front follows the retreating rains: the dry
north burns first (October-November), the parks at 5-8 degrees North burn
December-February.

How are the parks coping? Two numbers matter.

First: is burning inside each park going up or down? (Same satellite each
year, so the comparison is fair.)

    Chinko      down 68% from 2023 to 2025, then a 2026 rebound.
                The only park that clearly pushed fire out - for a while.
    Bili-Uere   flat, and high.
    Garamba     flat.
    Southern NP UP roughly 40% since 2023. The pressure is growing here.

Second: of the fires that enter each park, how many get stopped inside
rather than burning straight through?

    FIRES STOPPED INSIDE THE PARK

    Bili-Uere    27%  |###########
    Southern NP  22%  |#########     (2,112 fires crossed unstopped)
    Chinko       18%  |#######         (173 fires crossed unstopped)
    Garamba       6%  |##              (709 fires crossed unstopped)

Garamba is the outlier: 94 of every 100 fires that enter it burn through,
staying an average of 20 days. That is a management question, not a data
question.

One more useful detail: each park's fires arrive from a different side.
Chinko's come from the west, Bili-Uere's from the west and south, Garamba's
from the east. Different herder communities on each side - so each park
needs its own conversation, with its own neighbours.


2. FORESTS - LESS LOSS THAN IT LOOKS, AND TWO VERY DIFFERENT PROBLEMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Total forest loss since 2001: 1,167 km2, in 7,815 separate clearing events.
That sounds like a lot; it is 0.24% of the area in 25 years. By regional
standards, this is a low-deforestation landscape.

    FOREST LOSS BY YEAR (km2)

    2014 |#####                52
    2017 |##########          100
    2021 |######               60
    2022 |######               59
    2023 |################### 314   << mostly questioned data, see below
    2024 |                      1   (measuring method changes here -
    2025 |                      2    numbers before and after this line
    2026 |###                  30    must not be compared)

THE 2023 SPIKE IS PROBABLY NOT REAL. Almost all of it (282 of 314 km2) is a
handful of "logging" blocks flagged as suspect by the system itself, in one
remote corner in the far north. Three independent lines of evidence say
artefact, not logging boom:
  - Century-old survey maps show that exact ground as uninhabited dune
    country crossed by hunters' trails - one is even named "the elephant
    road". No settlement then.
  - Today's settlement data shows barely anyone there now.
  - The farmland satellite record shows only 13% of that ground was ever
    farmed - and it was farmed BEFORE 2023, so the "clearing" opened
    nothing. A real 280 km2 logging operation leaves marks on the land.
    This one left none.

THE REAL FOREST STORY is further south, along the Mbomou river on the
CAR-DRC border: active slash-and-burn farming pressing into border forests
near Zemio (5.0245 N 25.1290 E) and Doruma (4.7175 N 27.6805 E). The
three biggest clearings of 2026 (about 4 km2 each, made of hundreds of
small patches, saturated with fire) are all here.

AND HERE IS THE NEW INSIGHT - what happens to land AFTER the trees fall?
A new satellite farmland layer lets us check. The answer splits the region
in two:

    WHAT BECOMES FARMLAND AFTER FOREST LOSS?

    South (below 9.5 N):  648 km2 lost  ->  0.8% became farms
    North (above 9.5 N):   17 km2 lost  -> 46.1% became farms

Two completely different problems. In the south, forest is burned, used
briefly, and left to regrow - big scary numbers, but the land mostly comes
back. In the north, HALF of everything cleared stays farmland forever -
small numbers, but every hectare is a one-way door. The south generates the
alarming statistics; the north generates the irreversible ones.

Among the parks: Bili-Uere carries the most loss by far (602 km2, and it is
the wettest, most rainforest-like park - loss there does not recover).
Chinko is near-pristine: 6.5 km2 total in 25 years. Garamba is worsening
but tiny (worst year: 3.2 km2).


3. PEOPLE - THE GREAT RETURN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The region holds 2,121 settlements with about 2.6 million people. Their
actual built footprint - roofs and walls as the satellite measures them -
is 110 km2, about the size of Paris.

Who lives here, and for how long?

    WHAT KIND OF SETTLEMENTS?          HOW OLD ARE THEY?

    small camps   1,374  65%           built before 2000   1,665  79%
    towns           333  16%           built 2000-2015       208  10%
    villages        311  15%           built after 2015      248  12%
    other           103   5%

FINDING ONE: THE "CAMPS" ARE NOT TEMPORARY. 97% of the small "camps"
already existed in 2000. This is a permanent lattice of cattle-camps - and
maps drawn in the 1930s show the same system, in the same places, with the
surveyors' own glossary of Dinka cattle-camp words written on the sheets.
The old camps also sit deepest in the fire: the burning is the long-
standing pastoral economy, not something new arriving.

FINDING TWO: THE FABRIC IS MOSTLY NEW ANYWAY. 58% of all built surface
standing today was added after 2015. Not new places - the same places,
growing fast. 22% of the region's population now lives in settlements that
did not exist in 2015, concentrated in one belt in Western Bahr el Ghazal
(South Sudan), around Wau and the Busseri river.

FINDING THREE - and this changes everything about how to talk to these
communities: IT IS A RETURN, NOT AN INVASION. We cross-checked the new
settlements against the century-old survey maps. More than half of the new
settlements sit within 3 km of a village that was mapped and NAMED in the
1930s - more than twice what chance would give. The fastest growers:

    GROWTH     TODAY'S SPOT (coordinates)             NAME ON 1930s MAP
    +299 ha    Wau           (7.7110 N 27.9830 E)     "WAU"
    +160 ha    Koko, new town (9.2205 N 26.8570 E)    "Rian Kuj"
    +149 ha    town          (8.7300 N 27.4090 E)     "E. Kome"
    +138 ha    near Rumbek   (6.8395 N 29.6820 E)     "Mako"
    +108 ha    town          (9.0370 N 27.6210 E)     "Pan Nyel"
    + 93 ha    town          (8.2615 N 27.9485 E)     "Agork"
    + 92 ha    new town      (9.0320 N 27.5215 E)     "Win"

21 of the 30 fastest-growing settlements sit on named 1930s villages.
People displaced by decades of war are going home. These are returning
communities with historic claims - not encroachers. That is the right
frame for every land conversation in this belt.

FINDING FOUR: THE BOOM TOWNS DO NOT FEED THEMSELVES YET. Around most
settlements in the region, farmland has doubled since 2000 (from 4% to 9%
of nearby land). But around the booming return-belt towns, farmland is
still almost absent - about 1.5%. People have arrived; fields have not.
Compare the far north, where farming came FIRST (farmland around northern
settlements: 39%, and 63% around the newest ones).

What this predicts: the next wave of forest-to-farm conversion will open
around Rian Kuj, Win, Pan Nyel and Karyat - where the people are - not
where today's farmland map is greenest. Land-use planning there is early,
not late. That is rare good news: there is still time to plan.

AND BECAUSE EVERY SETTLEMENT NOW CARRIES ITS OWN HISTORY - its measured
built surface in 2000, in 2015 and today - three more facts fall out:

  * NOBODY IS LEAVING. Of 2,121 settlements, NOT ONE has lost even a
    third of its built fabric since 2000, while 764 grew by more than a
    third. Whatever the wars did to these places, abandonment is not in
    the satellite record - anywhere.

  * GROWTH IS ACCELERATING, NOT SETTLING. Total built surface was 26 km2
    in 2000, 47 km2 in 2015, 110 km2 today: the region built about four
    times faster per year after 2015 than before it. This is not a
    recovery levelling off; it is still speeding up.

  * THE CHAMPION IS A RETURN TOWN. Koko (9.2205 N 26.8570 E) measured
    3 hectares in 2000 - a hamlet. Today it is 209 hectares, sixty times
    larger, on the exact spot the 1930s sheet names "Rian Kuj". The
    region's largest single footprint remains Wau (7.7110 N 27.9830 E)
    at nearly 10 km2 - itself up 70% since 2000.

Inside the parks, settlement stays 2-3 times sparser than outside. Chinko
is the extreme case: 20 settlements, 826 people, in a park nearly the
size of Switzerland - with its only pressure point at the south-west
corner (around 6.2 N 23.3 E).


4. MINING - SMALL, SPECIFIC, AND INVISIBLE TO SATELLITES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
First, honesty about the instrument: we tried to DETECT artisanal mines
from satellite imagery and failed - the detector performed at chance and
was retired. What remains is REPORTED mines: 96 known sites in this region,
from incident reports and field lists.

Two clusters matter:

  * CHINKO'S SOUTH-WEST CORNER. A reported gold-and-diamond working sits
    essentially ON the park boundary at Kpangou (6.1770 N 23.3400 E),
    with several more within
    25 km. This is the same corner where the park's only settlement
    pressure sits - and those pits are in headwaters that drain INTO the
    park's rivers. Small, specific, and reachable by patrol.

  * THE UPPER KOTTO RIVER (north of Chinko). A dense field of workings
    around the town of Sam-Ouandja (8.5285 N 23.2510 E) - Yangou Dacko,
    Yangou Issi, the Kotto sites - each on the list BECAUSE an armed
    group attacked it. The villages here are literally named after the
    industry: Kotto 2, 3 and 4 (8.3575-8.4685 N along 23.34-23.39 E),
    and Yangou-Diamant, "diamond river" (8.5285 N 23.2290 E) [k]. This
    is armed-mining ground by definition, in the prefecture with the
    region's heaviest conflict record (818 armed events, 1,318 deaths
    since 1996).

How long has this been going on? The century-old maps answer: one site,
"Hofrat el Nahas" - Arabic for "the copper pit" - is printed on the
colonial sheets at 9.7610 N 24.3200 E; 8 km away (9.7840 N 24.2505 E) is
a modern mine where an armed attack was reported in 2016. The same pit,
worked and fought over, a century apart.
And it is not alone: two gold mines mapped today on OpenStreetMap
(9.3750 N and 9.3610 N at 24.1015 E) sit ONE kilometre from a well the
1930s surveyors named "Bir Andem" (9.3645 N 24.1100 E) - modern diggings
at a century-old watering point [e][f].
Extraction here is older than every armed group present.

One more cluster, just outside the frame: ten mapped mine pits sit INSIDE
Bili-Uere park, around the hamlet of Buza (4.0540 N 24.1340 E), a few
kilometres south of the study area's edge [f][k]. Apart from Chinko's
sites, it is the only mapped mining inside any of the four parks.

Two things we PROVED we cannot see, which is worth as much as a finding:

  * Mines have NO fire signature. Fire density near mines is the same as
    anywhere else.
  * Mines have NO lights. We measured night-time satellite radiance at 71
    mining sites: the median is zero - 50 times below what a lit village
    emits. THE MINES ARE DARK.

So: no satellite we operate can find or monitor these pits. A quiet sensor
does not mean a quiet place. Watching the two clusters is field work.

Can the mines poison the parks' water? We cannot measure pollution. What
we CAN say is the geometry: the south-west cluster sits upstream of
Chinko. If contamination happens, it flows in.

One warning about pretty maps, and one honest use for them. Our geology
layer offers two ways to look for gold, and they are NOT equally good [i]:

  * The COLOURED ROCK AREAS ("rocks that can host gold") fail here.
    Tested against real mine locations in eastern CAR they perform WORSE
    than random ground. Do not send patrols to amber shapes.

  * The CONTACT LINES - where two different rock types meet - carry a
    real, but disputed, signal. Against the 2019 field survey (675 mines)
    the gold-graded junctions concentrate known workings about 2.2-2.4
    times over random ground. But a second, smaller test list (a 2021
    imagery census, 32 mines inside mining permits) scores the same lines
    at 0.06x - effectively zero. Mixed evidence, and the app says so.
    Treat the lines as "where to point imagery and questions next" -
    never as a treasure map.

WITH THAT SAID: WHERE THE LINES RUN, BY NAME. The study area holds about
1,800 km of gold-graded contact lines [i], and the strongest type present
is the classic "greenstone" contact - ancient ocean-floor rock against the
metamorphic basement - forming one long broken arc through eastern CAR.
Several reported mining sites sit strikingly close to it. Walking the arc
south-west to north-east, with today's village names [k] and the 1930s
names where the old sheets reach [e]:

  * CHINKO'S WESTERN EDGE (5.4-6.0 N). About 100 km of the arc crosses
    the park's west, 25-35 km beyond the hamlets of Barogo (5.2800 N
    23.7185 E), Drouaza (5.3520 N 23.6155 E) and Prouazo Kalambour
    (5.3495 N 23.6225 E). This is the Kpangou corner again: two attacked
    sites near Yalinga (6.5100 N 23.2500 E) sit 3 and 7 km off the
    line, the reported working by Fode village (5.4885 N 23.3205 E)
    9 km. The rocks, the pits and the settlement pressure all point at
    the same corner of the same park.
    https://five-megapixel-conservation.exe.xyz/s/g-f5kkqmkc8z8fx8nx

  * THE DEMBIA-KARMADOR STRETCH (5.1-5.4 N, 24.5-24.9 E). Three more
    attacked-site clusters lie 3-15 km off the line, at villages with
    names: Dembia (5.1100 N 24.4675 E), Karmador (5.3760 N 24.6120 E),
    Zemoye (5.3980 N 24.6095 E) and Modoue (5.3145 N 24.5645 E).

  * THE BAROUA SITE. The one reported gold mine in the area's interior
    (5.6020 N 24.6900 E) sits ONE kilometre from the contact line, 2 km
    from Baroua village (5.5990 N 24.7080 E). If the line predicts
    anything, it looks like this.

  * THE MBOMOU STRETCH (around 6 N, 25.5-26.1 E). ~270 km of line in
    inhabited country - Kparatio (6.0185 N 25.5600 E), Ourou (6.0450 N
    25.3405 E) and Ouando (5.9605 N 25.9585 E) villages today; the 1930s
    sheets already name Rou and Rafi (5.94 N 25.55 E) and Kaka (6.0510 N
    26.0045 E) here.

  * THE EMPTY MIDDLE (7.0-7.9 N, 24.0-25.0 E). The longest share of the
    arc - roughly 700 km of line - crosses ground with NO modern named
    place at all: the nearest mapped village is 40+ km away. The only
    names this ground has ever had are on the 1930s sheets: Osso
    (7.6005 N 24.5885 E), Deye (7.5065 N 24.6385 E), Yango Asso
    (8.0670 N 24.4075 E). If anyone ever digs here, nobody will be watching.

  * THE KOTTO FIELD (8.2-8.6 N). The armed-mining cluster above sits
    11-22 km EAST of the arc, in the river alluvium downstream - exactly
    where gold washed out of a contact like this would end up. The map
    itself confesses the industry: villages named Kotto 2, 3 and 4 and
    Yangou-Diamant ("diamond river") ring the town of Sam-Ouandja; the
    1930s sheet marks "Kou" (8.5080 N 23.3415 E) at the same bend of the
    river.
    https://five-megapixel-conservation.exe.xyz/s/g-qx4xq8c9qvf27p3b

  * OUANDA DJALLE (8.8-9.0 N). The arc's northern end passes 5 km from
    the town of Ouanda Djalle (8.9005 N 22.8025 E) - printed under the
    same name on the 1930s sheets, beside hill names still on today's
    map (J. Gawarra, 8.7495 N 23.6410 E). Named,
    inhabited, reachable: the one stretch of the line that is watchable
    from the ground today.

Use the geology only as a hint for where to point imagery next - and use
the reported-site clusters for patrols.


5. THE HUNDRED-YEAR-OLD MAPS - AN UNEXPECTED INSTRUMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We digitised the complete Anglo-Egyptian Sudan Survey (187 map sheets,
drawn mostly in the 1930s) and machine-read every label: 94,300 names,
17,553 of them inside this region. Three things they turned out to hold:

  * THE FIRE MAP OF THE 2020s, DRAWN IN THE 1930s. The surveyors mapped
    the cattle-camp system - even writing the Dinka vocabulary on the
    sheets - and their grazing floodplains are exactly where today's fire
    front peaks. The burning geography is at least a century old.

  * THE NAMES OF THE BLANK SPACES. Where one 1930s sheet shows ~400 named
    villages, today's maps show 23. The returning settlements of section 3
    are landing on precisely these mapped, named, "vanished" villages. For
    the emptiest ground in the region, the best gazetteer is 90 years old.

  * A PRE-CONFLICT MINING REGISTER. "The copper pit", "Old copper
    workings", "Iron mines" - mineral sites recorded decades before any
    current armed group existed, for exactly the ground where modern
    reporting is thinnest.

We also tested the old geography against today's fire statistically: the
century-old caravan routes still carry slightly more burning than random
ground (+7%), and the old ironstone plains burn +20%. The fire is
structured by terrain and old human geography - not by new settlement.

(Handle with care: these sheets cover the Sudanese two-thirds of the
region only, the place names are machine-read from old print, and the
positioning is good to a few hundred metres.)


6. WHAT TO DO - SIX ACTIONS, IN ORDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. ASK GARAMBA THE 6% QUESTION. It stops one fire in sixteen; its
   neighbours stop one in four. This is the corridor's weakest number and
   it needs a management answer, not more data.

2. TREAT THE NORTH AS THE FRONT - AND THE PEOPLE AS RETURNEES. Southern
   NP's burning is up 40%; the return-belt holds 569,000 people in
   settlements younger than ten years. But they are going HOME, to villages
   with names on 90-year-old maps, and their farms have not arrived yet.
   Plan land use with them NOW, while the conversion front is still coming.

3. WATCH CHINKO'S SOUTH-WEST CORNER IN PERSON. Gold and diamond workings
   on the boundary, upstream of the park's water, invisible to every
   satellite. Only patrols and commissioned imagery can see it.

4. DO NOT QUOTE THE 2023 DEFORESTATION SPIKE. 280 of its 314 km2 is
   flagged, contradicted by the historic maps, the settlement record and
   the farmland record. Adjudicate it before it appears in anyone's slide.

5. KEEP THE KOTTO MINING CLUSTER ON THE CONFLICT DESK, not the geology
   desk. Every site there is known because it was attacked. And remember
   the pits are older than the armed groups that tax them.

6. PUT EVERYTHING IN THE JANUARY WINDOW. Fire arrives at every park in
   January; 87% of the year's burning is Nov-Feb. The engagement calendar
   is Dec 15 - Feb 15 - and the old maps even name the dry-season water
   points where the herds will be.


READING THE NUMBERS HONESTLY (short version)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
* Fire trends compare the same satellite year-on-year (the fleet tripled
  in 2024; raw counts across that line mislead).
* Forest-loss measurement changed method in 2024; numbers before and
  after are not comparable, so we never compare them.
* "Farmland" is what the satellite maps as stable cropland: pasture and
  brief shifting cultivation do not count. Zero farmland around a cattle
  camp is the definition working, not empty land.
* Settlement ages come from satellite building records (2000 / 2015 /
  today). A tiny 2015 camp below the satellite's threshold would read
  "new" - so "new" counts are upper bounds.
* Mine locations are REPORTED, mostly because of attacks - quiet mines in
  unreported areas are missing from every list.
* The 1930s map names are machine-read: verify against the original sheet
  image before citing one.
* Coordinates are given to about 50 m. That is the precision of OUR
  database point, not of the place: a village is hundreds of metres wide,
  and 1930s-map positions are only good to a few hundred metres.
